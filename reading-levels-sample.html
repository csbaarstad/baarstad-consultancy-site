Level 1Grades 2–3

An octopus is a sea animal with eight arms. It has no bones inside its body, so it is an invertebrate. When a hungry predator swims close, the octopus can hide. It uses camouflage to change its color and match the rocks and sand. Then the predator swims right past.

Check

How does an octopus use camouflage to stay safe?

Level 2Grade 4

The octopus is one of the ocean's cleverest escape artists. Because it has no backbone, it is an invertebrate, and its soft body can squeeze into tiny spaces. To avoid a predator, the octopus uses camouflage, changing the color and even the texture of its skin in less than a second to blend into rock, coral, or sand.

Check

What two things can an octopus change about its skin to hide from a predator?

Level 3Grades 5–6

Few animals disappear as completely as the octopus. As an invertebrate, it has no bones to limit its shape, so it can flatten and flow into narrow cracks in the reef. Its real defense, though, is camouflage. Special cells in its skin let it shift color and texture almost instantly, so a passing predator sees nothing but rock and shadow.

Check

Why does being an invertebrate help the octopus escape a predator, and how does camouflage add to that defense?

Level 4Grades 7–8

The octopus is a master of disappearing. Lacking the skeleton of most animals, this invertebrate can reshape its boneless body to pour through openings barely wider than its eye. Its most striking ability, however, is camouflage: layered cells beneath the skin expand and contract to repaint the animal in under a second, matching not only the color but the texture of nearby rock, coral, or sand. To a circling predator, the octopus simply ceases to exist.

Check

The passage calls the octopus a “master of disappearing.” Which two abilities support that claim, and how does each one work?

Level 5Grades 9–10

Few creatures exploit their environment as ingeniously as the octopus. As an invertebrate, it is unconstrained by a rigid skeleton, allowing it to compress its entire body through gaps scarcely larger than its eye. Yet its most remarkable adaptation is camouflage. Specialized pigment cells, controlled directly by the nervous system, expand and contract to alter both color and surface texture in a fraction of a second, rendering the animal nearly indistinguishable from rock, coral, or sand. For a hunting predator, the octopus does not flee so much as vanish.
